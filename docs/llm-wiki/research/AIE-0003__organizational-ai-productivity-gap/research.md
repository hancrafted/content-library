# Why do organizations fail to reach the promised AI productivity boost?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that buying or rolling out AI does not by itself produce organization-level productivity gains, and to say what the gap consists of. Organizational ROI figures are contested, so the question separates what the studies measured from how they are quoted.

## Answer

Most organizations have adopted AI, yet measured firm-level effects remain small: a 2026 survey of about 6,000 executives found roughly 90% reporting no impact on productivity or employment over the prior three years. The best-supported explanation is that AI is a general purpose technology whose value depends on complementary, largely intangible investment in new processes, skills and organization, which shows up late and is poorly measured. The much-quoted claim that "95% of pilots fail" is a disputed figure: it rests on interviews and leader surveys rather than company reporting, success was judged by the respondents themselves, and the report calls its figures "directionally accurate", and a Wharton survey of about 800 enterprise leaders reports the opposite picture (about three in four see positive ROI), though also self-reported. What both sides agree on is that tools are not integrated into workflows and that training and skill-building lag usage.

## Findings

1. General purpose technologies such as AI "enable and require significant complementary investments", such as new processes, business models and human capital. These investments are often intangible and poorly measured, so productivity is understated early and shows up later, a "Productivity J-Curve" [1].
2. In a survey of close to 6,000 senior executives in the US, UK, Germany and Australia, about 69% of firms actively use AI, yet roughly 90% of executives report no impact on employment or productivity at their firms over the last three years. Executives themselves use AI about 1.5 hours a week on average, and forecast a 1.4% productivity gain over the next three years [2].
3. Linking adoption surveys to Danish administrative records, researchers (paper now titled "Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI") find precise null effects of chatbots on earnings and recorded hours, ruling out effects larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT's launch, although most employers in exposed occupations have adopted chatbot initiatives. Employers absorbed AI through task reorganization that created new work in content generation, AI oversight and AI integration [3].
4. The MIT NANDA report "The GenAI Divide" states that 95% of organizations get "zero return" and that only 5% of integrated pilots extract millions in value. It attributes the gap to a "learning gap": tools that do not learn, integrate poorly or fit workflows, rather than to model quality or regulation [4].
5. The dispute: the 95% is self-graded by the people interviewed or surveyed: success means workloads that "users or executives have remarked as causing a marked and sustained productivity and/or P&L impact", not audited results. The report's own limitations say its figures are "directionally accurate based on individual interviews rather than official company reporting", that success definitions may differ across organizations, and that the sample is 52 interviews, 153 conference-collected leader surveys and 300 public initiatives. It defines success as deployment beyond pilot with measurable KPIs, measured six months after the pilot, and concedes that period "may be insufficient" and may understate success. It also concedes selection bias and that its build-versus-buy difference may reflect organizational capability rather than approach [4]. The executive summary states the figure as "95% of organizations" while the same sentence's second half counts "integrated AI pilots", so the unit is ambiguous [4].
6. Strongest counter-evidence: a Wharton and GBK survey of about 800 US enterprise decision-makers (October 2025) reports that nearly three-quarters already see positive ROI, that 72% formally track ROI metrics and that four in five expect positive returns within two to three years. It is also self-reported, and VP-level and above respondents are more positive than mid-managers (81% versus 69%) [5].
7. The same Wharton report points at the human side: nearly half of organizations report technical skill gaps while investment in training has softened (down 8 points) and confidence in training as the path to fluency is down 14 points; 43% of leaders warn of skill atrophy without deliberate role design, coaching and time to practice [5].
8. The NANDA report also finds individuals succeeding where organizations stall: workers at over 90% of surveyed companies reported regular use of personal AI tools while official initiatives stalled [4].

## Caveats

The Answer's causal link, that missing complementary investment explains today's gap, is inference: the J-Curve paper is a historical and theoretical argument and finds AI-related intangible capital effects "small but growing", and the NANDA "learning gap" is an interview-based interpretation. The Danish result concerns chatbots and earnings and hours, not firm output. The NANDA source [4] is a third-party copy of the NANDA PDF: the original address named by secondary sources (nanda.media.mit.edu/ai_report_2025.pdf) redirected to the NANDA group page on 2026-10-13 and no MIT-hosted copy was found, so the copy's match to the original is unverified. The quoted passages were checked against that copy's extracted text on 2026-10-13. The NANDA report is not peer-reviewed. Both the NANDA and Wharton figures are self-reported by participants, so neither settles the true share of organizations gaining.
