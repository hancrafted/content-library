# Why do typical B2B sales cycles take as long as they do?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that a B2B deal needs months between first contact and a signed contract, so the work that creates revenue must start well before the money is needed. The claim concerns what makes the buyer's decision slow, not what a sales team can do to speed it up.

## Answer

A B2B sale is slow because the buyer is not one person but a group, and each member can block the purchase, so the seller must build agreement across people with different roles and priorities. Peer-reviewed work on organizational purchasing finds that the time a firm takes to decide is significantly affected by the size of its decision-making unit and by the type of purchase (without giving a direction here), and survey work shows buying groups of five to sixteen people who often disagree. There is no single typical cycle length: it depends on deal complexity and group size, so any one worked example is an illustration, not a benchmark.

## Findings

1. In an empirical study of firms buying telecommunications systems, the length of time a firm takes to reach a major purchase decision was significantly affected by the type of purchase (buyclass), firm size, decision-making unit size, information sources used and size of the consideration set [1].
2. Sellers rarely find one person who can approve a deal alone, whether the customer has 50 employees or 50,000. Decision authority rests with a group whose members have different roles and all have veto power [2].
3. The authors of that article describe reaching consensus and closing deals as an increasingly painful and protracted process, for buyers and sellers alike [2].
4. A Gartner survey of 632 B2B buyers describes buying groups of five to 16 people across as many as four functions, each member with differing priorities and opinions [3].
5. In that survey 74% of buyer teams showed unhealthy conflict, meaning conflicting objectives, disagreement on the best course of action, or being overruled by external decision-makers. Groups that reach consensus are 2.5 times more likely to report a high-quality deal [3].
6. In a Gartner survey of more than 1,000 B2B customers, 89% found the information they met during purchase to be of high quality, yet buyers reported being overwhelmed by its volume and by contradictions among suppliers. Customers who met too much contradictory information were 153% more likely to settle for a smaller, less disruptive course of action than planned [4].

## Caveats

No source read for this file measures a typical cycle length in months, or the split of that time between lead, opportunity and deal. The Findings establish why cycles are long and what drives their length, not how long they are. The Answer's statement that cycle length has no single typical value is an inference from findings 1 and 4, where group size and purchase type drive decision time.

The worked example the Brief's source deck uses (lead 2 weeks, opportunity 6 months, deal 3 months, about 9.5 months in total) is unverified by any source here and is traceable only to a third-party graphic. Treat it as an illustration of a complex sale, not as a typical value.

The decision-time study reports only the abstract-level result that the five antecedents significantly affect decision time. The full text is behind a paywall, so its effect sizes and direction for each antecedent were not read. That the study covered telecommunications purchases of the early 1990s limits how far its numbers transfer to software and services today. The Harvard Business Review page was read only in part, so the group-size figures the article may give were not used. The Gartner group-size range is one survey's, and published ranges differ between Gartner releases, so the range should be quoted with its survey and not as a constant.
