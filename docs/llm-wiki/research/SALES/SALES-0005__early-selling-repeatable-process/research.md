# Why does engaging in sales early lead to a repeatable sales process?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that selling before revenue is urgent pays off in a process, not only in cash. The question is about how a sales process comes to exist, so it is scoped to the learning that early selling produces.

## Answer

A repeatable sales process cannot be designed in advance; it is found by selling. Early deals, closed by the founders themselves, show who buys, why, through which steps and how long it takes, and that knowledge is what can later be written down and handed to others. Skipping this and hiring or scaling first multiplies a process nobody has yet understood. Starting early leaves room for more attempts at finding the process before the money runs out, which is an inference from the fact that models usually change.

## Findings

1. A startup is an organization formed to search for a repeatable and scalable business model, so finding a repeatable model is the task, not a given [1].
2. The signal that a model has been found is that revenue, users or traffic start increasing in a repeatable way the founders predicted. Even the first time that happens, the model found may not be the optimal one [1].
3. In Blank's Customer Development course, the goal of the validation stage is to develop a repeatable sales process. Its exit criteria ask whether there is a proven sales roadmap, whether the sales cycle is understood (average selling price, lifetime value, return on investment), and whether there is a set of orders [3].
4. The same slides criticise the traditional launch plan, where hiring a sales VP and first sales staff is scheduled against product development rather than against anything learned from customers [3].
5. Blank's case of a company that scaled its sales force on early orders from friends and board connections: none of its leaders knew what a repeatable sales model would look like, and the company had not understood how and why customers would buy [2].
6. Blank's stated lessons from that case: premature scaling of sales and marketing is the leading cause of cash loss in a startup; scale after the founders and a small team have found a repeatable sales model; early sales from board members or friends are good for morale and cash but may not show what the business model is [2].
7. Direct engagement with the earliest customers gives feedback Graham calls the best a founder will ever get, and corrects the founders' initial model of users, which is always inaccurate [4].
8. Graham's advice is to start by recruiting users manually and gradually move to less manual methods: founders who do the work by hand know exactly what to build because of the muscle memory from doing it, and can automate the bottlenecks one at a time [4].
9. Habits formed while customers are few persist: working hard to delight a handful of users is what a company keeps doing at scale [4].
10. Blank writes that most startups change their business model at least once, if not several times, so the first model is rarely the one that lasts [1].

## Caveats

The Findings are practitioner and teaching sources, not controlled studies; they are widely used frameworks, not measured results. The step from "learning from early deals" to "a repeatable process" is Blank's model, and Graham writes about recruiting users rather than B2B sales specifically, so applying his points to a sales process is an inference. The Answer's last sentence is also an inference: the Findings show that business models usually change and that premature scaling burns cash, but no source states that an earlier start yields more iterations before cash runs out. Blank's slides are the source of the validation goals and exit criteria; the course slides are a teaching document, not a peer-reviewed work. Thresholds often quoted elsewhere (a number of customers or revenue before a first sales hire) were found only in secondary summaries and are deliberately not used.
