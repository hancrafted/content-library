# Why does revenue typically lag the costs that generate it in an early-stage startup?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that a startup's cash position is decided by timing, not only by how much it eventually earns. The question is scoped to the sequence of cost and revenue events inside one early-stage company.

## Answer

Costs start the day the company does: legal setup, living expenses, product work and hiring all come before the first customer. Revenue comes only after a chain of later steps: finding customers, closing them, invoicing, and waiting out payment terms. Each step adds delay, so cash goes out months before it comes back, and a company can run out of money while its eventual revenue is still on the way. Because this lag is structural, the time to start the revenue chain is well before the cash is needed.

## Findings

1. A startup's first expenses, such as company setup and the founders' living costs, arise before it has users or income [2].
2. A high burn rate puts a startup under constant time pressure, and spending little while working fast is the stated remedy [2].
3. Money that is promised does not arrive at once: finding investors takes time, closing takes longer than expected even after a yes, and one angel took months to pay [2].
4. The same delay applies to a customer's money, not only an investor's: with no contract term, interest on a late B2B payment starts only 30 days after the invoice is received, and contract terms may run up to 60 days [3].
5. An acceptance or verification step can add up to 30 more days before the payment period starts [3].
6. Late payment is recognised in law as harming liquidity and complicating financial management, so delay between work and cash is a known cash-flow risk [3].
7. Hiring ahead of results raises expenses without a matching rise in revenue; there is little connection between how much a startup spends and how fast it grows [1].
8. Revenue that is only predicted is discounted by investors, and founders who rely on raising more money often find that assumption false, so projected revenue does not cover present costs [1].
9. The test of whether a company reaches profitability on its remaining money, at current expenses and growth, gives the timeline in which a lag becomes fatal; worrying too early is cheap and worrying too late is dangerous [1].

## Caveats

The sources are practitioner essays and one statute, not a measured study of startup cash cycles. They show each delay separately; that they add up into one lag, and that sales must start early to shorten it, is an inference. The Directive binds EU member states only, and other jurisdictions have other payment norms. The length of sales cycles, a main part of the lag, is covered by a separate question and not argued here.
