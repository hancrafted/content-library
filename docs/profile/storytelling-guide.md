# Han's storytelling

How Han builds a story. Shape the Dossier's Sections and every Slide's Voice script with this method. Examples show the move. Write fresh lines from the Episode's own material.

## The General flow

This is the spine: sell a vision from the top down, and show competence from the bottom up. It is the Trust budget from the mental-model guide, told as a story.

1. **Build connection (the initial why and how).** Anchor a human bond before any fact. Use the strongest kind the material allows:
   1. a personal story (vulnerability)
   2. a shared pain
   3. a shared vision
   4. a shared ideology, as a last resort
2. **Claim (the what).** Spend the trust you have built on a speculation or extrapolation that rests on the anchor.
3. **Maintain trust (the how).** Tell a relevant story whose facts or data prove competence. It backs the last claim, prepares the next one, or both.
4. **Repeat** steps 2 and 3. When trust runs low, or a new pain opens, go back to step 1.

The flow works at two scales:

5. **The Episode.** The Hook builds connection. Each Section makes one claim, and its Slides maintain trust. A Section that opens a new pain starts with a fresh connection.
   - Example: Hook "Imagine you're onboarding a new colleague…". The Section claims that maintenance, not writing, is now the cost. Its Slides prove it with the effort curve and the compounding references.
   - Not this: an opening on the market size, the product or the speaker's credentials.

6. **The Slide.** One Slide's Voice script tells its own short story: a relatable way in, the claim, then the fact or case that backs it.
   - Example: "Here's a markdown file, an onboarding process." → "A machine can check the shape of that metadata." → "It can tell you the field exists. It can't tell you whether what's written in it is true."
   - Not this: a Slide that lists facts and never claims anything.

## Principles

7. **Golden Circle.** Tell the listener why it matters before you say what it is. Facts are the furthest point from a human bond, so they come last.
   - Example: "Before we start, I want to establish why markdown files matter so much."
   - Not this: "Today: three categories of markdown files."

8. **Anchoring and priming.** The first thing the listener hears frames everything after it and points where the story is going. Open with an "imagine…" scenario in the second person, built on the Analogy.
   - Example: "Imagine you're assigned a freelancer with some really weird characteristics."
   - Not this: a definition as the first line.

9. **Pain first.** A shared pain bonds more strongly than a shared hope. Let the listener feel the problem before you name the fix.
   - Example: the lonely widower shown before the product.
   - Not this: "Our solution reduces emissions by 17%."

10. **Claim–proof separation.** The listener always knows which sentence is a claim and which is evidence. Unproven claims get calibrated candour, as the tone guide describes.
    - Example: "One thing I want to be straight about. The middle region is my hypothesis."
    - Not this: a guess stated with the same certainty as a measured fact.

11. **Chained transitions.** Each segment ends on the line the next one picks up, and the story moves on "so" and "but".
    - Example: "…That brake is gone." → "So the brake is gone. Which raises the obvious question…"
    - Not this: "Next, let's look at…"

12. **Ladder of abstraction.** Lift a concrete case into the general principle, then bring it back down to something the listener can picture.
    - Example: one stale document, then "Now scale that", then the compounding corpus.
    - Not this: a whole Section spent in the abstract.

13. **Practitioner proof.** Han's own coaching and building work is the strongest evidence: what he did, what happened, and his share in the result.
    - Example: "Let me show you how I did it with another team I coached last year."
    - Not this: a hypothetical where a real case exists.

## Optional moves

Use each one when its condition holds.

14. **Callback**, when one diagram carries the whole argument: bring the same picture back with a new meaning.
    - Example: "This is the third time you've seen this picture."
15. **Signposting**, when a Section walks through several parts: name the route before taking it.
    - Example: "Left to right. Let's take them in order."
16. **Peak-end close**, on the final Slide: recap the main points, give an honest caveat and make a low-key offer.
    - Example: "The tool's here if you want it. It's early. Thank you."
