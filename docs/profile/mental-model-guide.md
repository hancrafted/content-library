# Han's mental model

How Han reasons. Build an Episode's argument from these models, so every claim follows a line he would draw. Examples show the move. Write fresh lines from the Episode's own material.

## Core models

1. **Backward chaining.** Start from the tangible outcome and break it down until the next step is fully within your control. Whatever stays outside your control becomes a numbers game, such as a funnel or a conversion rate. Plan forward only while the goal is too vague to plan back from, and name the moment you switch.
   - Example: "What do I need in order to accomplish this milestone?"
   - Not this: tasks listed in the order they came to mind.

2. **Trade-offs.** Quality, cost and speed always trade against each other, and the measure is outcome per input. Turn every trade-off into an informed decision.
   - Example: "Using a premium model for everything is like using your best engineer to bring you coffee."
   - Not this: "Always use the best tool."

3. **Trust budget.** Evidence earns trust, and claims spend it. That includes true claims the listener cannot check. Documents earn trust the same way, through provenance and verification. The General flow in the storytelling guide is this model told as a story.
   - Example: "Every claim uses up the trust meter you're building."
   - Not this: three bold claims before any proof.

4. **Translation layers.** Software, prompts and tools translate human intent into raw computing power. Each new technology creates a discipline for coping with it: prompt engineering, then context engineering, then harness engineering.
   - Example: "If your customer could write SQL straight to the database, they wouldn't need a front end."
   - Not this: a new tool presented as magic.

5. **Second-order thinking.** Scale every case up and follow it down the line: what happens at a thousand, and what does that cause next? Show at least two alternatives, so the listener sees that there are many cases and a choice between them.
   - Example: "Now scale that… you're verifying every claim each file makes about the others. That compounds."
   - Not this: one example offered as the only way.

## How I view AI

6. **Determinism split.** The machine is deterministic, binary, cheap and fast, so give it as much as it can carry. The human makes the semantic judgment and is where trust comes from. AI is the fuzzy middle: it works in natural language, it is not deterministic, and how far it can be trusted is still open.
   - Example: "Left to right. Cheap and certain, to expensive and human."
   - Not this: "AI will verify it for you."

7. **Human accountability.** Tools narrow the work and stop where the human begins. Ownership of what is true stays with a person.
   - Example: "The machine narrows it. The AI narrows it further. What's left is the part that was always yours."
   - Not this: AI making the final call.

8. **Moving bottlenecks.** When AI makes one half of a job cheap, the cost moves to the other half and compounds there.
   - Example: "Writing was the brake. That brake is gone."
   - Not this: "AI saves time", with no word on where the time went.

9. **AI as a hire.** Picture the model as someone you hired, then ask how you would brief that person and budget for them.
   - Example: a freelancer who is fast, well read, tireless, never asks questions and forgets everything after each conversation.
   - Not this: the model described as a chatbot or an oracle.

## Startups

Use this section in Episodes about founders, pitching or fundraising.

10. **Buy-in.** Investors, judges, recruits and co-founders are each deciding whether to join the journey. Every part of a pitch serves that decision.
    - Example: "People feel like, I want to be part of your journey."
    - Not this: a deck that fills the scoring card with one slide per criterion.

11. **Market validation.** A paying customer is the strongest trust signal: it turns "can you sell it?" into "can you scale it?". A team that knows its conversion rate and sales cycle shows it knows its market.
    - Example: "Once you have your first paying customer, the question is about scaling."
    - Not this: technical brilliance offered as proof of demand.

12. **Founder time.** Time is a founder's scarcest resource. Spend it where the expected outcome is worth the effort.
    - Example: "If the winning team gets a closed-door meeting with an investor, that's a different set of priorities."
    - Not this: every opportunity prepared with equal effort.

13. **One champion.** A panel decides because at least one person fought for you. Treat the decision makers as personas and learn what each one cares about.
    - Example: an angel investor, a VC partner and a judge who is not an investor each want something different.
    - Not this: one pitch aimed at an average judge.

## Frequently used analogies

Use one when a concept needs a human frame. Each maps a technical idea onto something the listener already manages.

14. **The new colleague you onboard:** an agent needs the right information at the right time.
15. **The hired freelancer:** see AI as a hire (9).
16. **A stranger off the street:** what it means to let AI make the judgment call.
17. **Your best engineer bringing coffee:** a premium model spent on trivial work.
18. **The brake:** a constraint that held a system in check until it was removed.
19. **The trust meter:** see Trust budget (3); empty it and the house of cards falls.
20. **Fresh at nine, drained at five:** a context window filling up and losing attention.
21. **Building your dream home:** the relatable stand-in for building a software platform.
