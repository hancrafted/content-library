# Why it's never too early to start with sales {#never-too-early-sales}

## Why watching cashflow is mission critical {#why-watching-cashflow-is-mission-critical}

#### Voice script

1. **Imagine the wall** — Imagine you are the founder, standing in front of one wall. Sticky notes, one column per month, yellow for the cash you have, red for the costs, green for the revenue. You have watched a board like this, or you are about to, and you know the feeling when you read the last column.
2. **Cash decides how long you have** — Here is the claim of this whole Episode: what decides how long your company lives is not how much you earn in the end, but when the money arrives. This one board comes back three times, and each time it answers a bigger question.
3. **The route** — We will go through four questions. Why cashflow is mission critical, why sales is more than selling, how to start doing sales, and why a pipeline takes time to build. The first answer is already on the wall.

#### Speaker notes

1. **Costs begin before customers** — company setup and the founders' living costs arise before a startup has users or income [1].
   - sources: [1] Paul Graham, A Student's Guide to Startups: Startup Funding — https://www.paulgraham.com/startupfunding.html
2. **A high burn rate is a clock** — it puts a startup under constant time pressure, and the stated remedy is to spend little while working fast [1].
   - sources: [1] Paul Graham, A Student's Guide to Startups: Startup Funding — https://www.paulgraham.com/startupfunding.html
3. **The default-alive test** — ask whether the company reaches profitability on its remaining money at current expenses and growth; worrying too early is cheap, worrying too late is dangerous [2].
   - sources: [2] Paul Graham, Default Alive or Default Dead? — https://paulgraham.com/aord.html
   - target: the Cash €0 note in Feb

#### Voice script

1. **Walk the months** — Read it with me. January, you start with 100 thousand in cash and 50 thousand goes out. February, another 50 thousand goes out, and at the end of February your cash is zero.
2. **March has no money** — March arrives and the cost lands on an empty account, with nothing to pay it. The big number sits in April, one month late.
3. **Antithesis** — A million euros is coming, and the company is gone. Right on paper, dead on the calendar.
4. **Hand over the verdict** — So find your own February: the month your cash hits zero. And if that is the month you run out, when would you have needed to start selling?

## Why sales is more than just selling {#why-sales-is-more-than-just-selling}

#### Voice script

1. **Pain first** — You may think sales is something for later, when the money is needed. If so, you leave most of its value on the table, because the money is the last thing sales gives you.
2. **The claim** — My claim is this: selling early pays out before the revenue does. There are three payouts, and each one is a reason to start now.

### Sales is more than selling {#sales-is-more-than-selling}

Three boxes side by side with oversized numerals: "Gain market insight" (every customer talk yields research data: where to spend time, validated hypotheses, network), "Gain internal insights" (how effective are you, can you build a repeatable and scalable sales process: first sales hire, growing the org) and "Increase trust" (investors and customers: attracting investment, convincing reluctant customers). Arrows run from the insight boxes into "Increase trust", which is drawn heaviest, so the boxes read as converging on it. Hovering a box grows an accent line above it.

Design reference: reference/source-deck/design-reference.md#page-4

#### Speaker notes

1. **Your starting model is wrong** — a founder's initial model of users is always inaccurate, even when the founder is one of them, and direct contact with the earliest users gives the best feedback the company will get [1].
   - sources: [1] Graham, Do Things that Don't Scale — https://paulgraham.com/ds.html
   - target: the "Gain market insight" box
2. **Ask about the last time** — what yields insight is specifics: ask about problems the user already faced, not hypotheticals, and treat talking to customers as part of the CEO's job [2]. The sources are interviews and users, not sales calls; that a sales conversation yields the same facts is an inference.
   - sources: [2] Y Combinator (Migicovsky), Startup School Week 1 Recap: How to Talk to Users — https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/
   - target: the "Gain market insight" box
3. **A signal must be costly to fake** — when one side knows more than the other, an action that is cheaper for a strong type than a weak one carries information, because the cost carries it, not the content [3].
   - sources: [3] Spence, Job Market Signaling — https://doi.org/10.2307/1882010
   - target: the "Increase trust" box
4. **Investors read the team** — in a survey of 885 institutional venture capitalists the management team was the most-cited factor (95%), ahead of business model (83%), product (74%) and market (68%) [4].
   - sources: [4] Gompers, Gornall, Kaplan and Strebulaev, How Do Venture Capitalists Make Decisions? — https://www.nber.org/papers/w22587
   - target: the "Increase trust" box
5. **No study measures LOIs or pipelines** — carrying signaling theory over to early sales results is an inference, and a non-binding letter of intent is cheap to give, so it separates strong from weak firms poorly; the more binding the commitment, the more trust it plausibly earns [3].
   - sources: [3] Spence, Job Market Signaling — https://doi.org/10.2307/1882010
   - target: the "Increase trust" box
6. **Premature scaling burns cash** — Blank calls premature scaling of sales and marketing the leading cause of cash loss in a startup [5].
   - sources: [5] Blank, It Must Be A Marketing Problem — https://steveblank.com/2010/02/11/it-must-be-a-marketing-problem/
   - target: the "Gain internal insights" box
7. **Friends' orders can mislead** — early orders from board members or friends are good for morale and cash but may not show what the business model is [5].
   - sources: [5] Blank, It Must Be A Marketing Problem — https://steveblank.com/2010/02/11/it-must-be-a-marketing-problem/
   - target: the "Gain internal insights" box

#### Voice script

1. **Every talk is research** — Every customer conversation is market research you did not have to pay for. It tells you where to spend your time and which hypothesis to drop. This is my reading of selling as a conversation, not a measured result.
2. **Know how well you sell** — The second payout stays inside your company. You learn how well you sell, and whether anyone besides you could repeat it, because a repeatable process is what lets you hire the first salesperson.
3. **Trust is the sum** — The third is trust. Investors and reluctant customers trust what someone else has already bought. To be straight about it, that is a claim and not a proof: a first result shows that your team can sell, and that is why it counts.
4. **Verdict** — Insight and process both end in trust, so look at the three boxes and ask yourself which one your next investor would ask about first.

## How to start doing sales {#how-to-start-doing-sales}

#### Voice script

1. **Backward chaining** — Start from the end and walk backwards. The end is a signed contract, and you walk back until the next step is in your own hands, because do sales is too vague to start on a Monday.
2. **The claim** — Here is the claim: sales has three stages, leads, opportunities and closing. The first steps are ones you control, and only after them does it become a numbers game.
3. **Signposting** — We go left to right. First the stages, then a deal board to track them, then marketing, and then the cashflow board once more.

### Stages of sale {#stages-of-sale}

#### Visual

master: three-column · motion: none

Three boxes left to right, joined by arrows: "Leads" (lead generation: research and maintain a list of potential customers; initial contact: cold call, calling inbound leads, leverage your network), "Opportunities" (the potential customer shows interest; this is the actual hard work: follow-up, sales material, meetings, negotiations) and "Closing / Deal" (the customer agrees to a contractual and transactional relationship). A fainter fourth segment trails off the right edge, "After-sale: production, delivery, customer success", marked as more waiting before cash arrives.

Design reference: reference/source-deck/design-reference.md#page-6
Design reference: reference/source-deck/design-reference.md#page-7

#### Speaker notes

1. **Cash waits on paperwork** — with no contract term, interest on a late B2B payment starts only 30 days after the invoice is received, contract terms may run up to 60 days, and an acceptance step can add up to 30 more days before the clock starts. This is an EU directive; other jurisdictions differ [1].
   - sources: [1] European Parliament and Council, Directive 2011/7/EU on combating late payment in commercial transactions — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32011L0007
   - target: the After-sale segment
2. **The lags add up** — the sources show each delay separately: finding customers, closing, invoicing, payment terms. That they add into one lag, which is why the chain must start early, is an inference [1].
   - sources: [1] European Parliament and Council, Directive 2011/7/EU on combating late payment in commercial transactions — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32011L0007

#### Voice script

1. **Leads, step one** — The first step is lead generation. Research and keep a list of potential customers, with names, not a feeling. Start the list this week, and maintain it every week after that.
2. **First contact, step two** — The second step is first contact. Cold call, call back an inbound lead, or ask your network, and pick one of them. Make that first call, because that is what opens opportunities.
3. **Opportunities are the work** — An opportunity means interest, and interest is not a deal. Follow-up, material, meetings and negotiation are the hard part of sales, and they take calendar time that you cannot compress with willpower.
4. **Closing is not cash** — Even a signed contract is not cash. After it come production, delivery and customer success, and only then the invoice, so every stage pushes the money further to the right.

3. **Start small** — So start small. Track your deals in a simple pipeline, and know that a spreadsheet is enough. The tool is not the point, the habit is.

1. **Customers do not arrive alone** — "You can't wait for users to come to you. You have to go out and get them": getting users is a gradual process, and early numbers look too small only to those who underestimate compound growth [1].

2. **The marketing hand-off has its own rate** — in one vendor dataset the rate from marketing-qualified to sales-qualified lead was 68 percent, against a 19 percent win rate further down; stage rates are measured on one company's own stage definitions [2].

1. **Two ways in** — There are two ways to get a lead. You go and get it, or it walks in on its own, and marketing is the second way.
2. **Volume into a defined stage** — Marketing speaks to many people at once, so that a customer enters themselves into a defined stage. An inbound lead is one example, an e-commerce checkout is another.
3. **Hand to sales** — Marketing fills the stage and sales works what lands in it. Which means marketing does not replace the first call, it gives that call someone to be made to.

## Why building an effective sales pipeline takes time {#why-building-an-effective-sales-pipeline-takes-time}

#### Voice script

1. **The obvious objection** — So the obvious objection: I start selling in January, and the April revenue lands in March? It does not work like a switch.
2. **The claim** — A pipeline is built, measured and tuned, and that takes months. Three words describe it: funnel, conversion rate and sales cycle.

### What is a sales pipeline {#what-is-a-sales-pipeline}

#### Visual

master: grid-1x3 · motion: local

Three boxes side by side with oversized numerals: "Sales funnel" (defines each stage of a sales process; your org's value through volume per stage), "Conversion rate" (how many actions in one stage convert to one success in the next; your org's value through the attractiveness of your offering) and "Sales cycle" (how long from start to finish until you closed a deal or receive the money; your org's value through your efficiency). The words volume, offering and efficiency are set in the accent colour. Hovering a box grows an accent line above it.

Design reference: reference/source-deck/design-reference.md#page-10

#### Speaker notes

1. **Stage rates are not portable** — a stage's rate depends on where a vendor draws its start and end, so rates from different companies are likely not comparable stage by stage; in one dataset two stages already differ, 68 and 19 percent. This is an inference [1].
   - sources: [1] Ebsta and Pavilion, GTM Benchmarks Report — https://benchmarks.ebsta.com/hubfs/V3%202025%20Benchmark%20Report/gtm_benchmarks_digital_report.pdf
   - target: the "Conversion rate" box
2. **Cycle length hits cash** — a unit-economics model leaves out the sales cycle and the ramp time of a salesperson; their effect on cash flow has to be modelled separately [2].
   - sources: [2] Mark Roberge, The Science of Scaling — https://f.hubspotusercontent10.net/hubfs/6575667/Stage%202s%20Science%20of%20Scaling.pdf
   - target: the "Sales cycle" box

#### Voice script

1. **Funnel is volume** — The funnel names each stage of your sales process. How many enter each stage tells you how much volume you can put through.
2. **Conversion is the offer** — The conversion rate says how many actions in a stage become one success in the next. A low rate may be telling you that your offering is not yet attractive, and that is useful to know early.
3. **Cycle is efficiency** — The cycle is the time from start to finish, until the deal or the money. It is your efficiency, and with conversion that gives you two numbers you should measure from your first deal.

### Pipeline by the numbers {#pipeline-by-the-numbers}

#### Visual

master: basic-page · motion: local

A horizontal funnel of our own drawing in three segments: Lead (2 weeks), Opportunity (6 months), Deal (3 months). Above it, brackets show 3:1 from lead to opportunity, 9:1 from opportunity to deal and "Total conversion rate 27:1"; below it a timeline bar splits into 2 weeks, 6 months and 3 months under "Sales cycle: 9.5 months total". The segments draw in from the left, then the ratios and durations appear.

Design reference: reference/source-deck/design-reference.md#page-11

#### Speaker notes

1. **A reference win rate** — in a dataset of 655 thousand opportunities across 387 companies, the new-customer win rate was 19 percent (about 5.3 opportunities per win), the sales cycle 91 days and 8 stakeholders per deal; expansion sales to existing customers ran 45 percent, 52 days and 5 stakeholders. It is a vendor study of its own customers' CRM data [1].
   - sources: [1] Ebsta and Pavilion, GTM Benchmarks Report — https://benchmarks.ebsta.com/hubfs/V3%202025%20Benchmark%20Report/gtm_benchmarks_digital_report.pdf
2. **The biggest loss is no decision** — an analysis of more than 2.5 million recorded sales conversations found 40 to 60 percent of deals are lost to buyers who intend to buy but never act [2].
   - sources: [2] Dixon and McKenna, Stop Losing Sales to Customer Indecision (Harvard Business Review) — https://hbr.org/2022/06/stop-losing-sales-to-customer-indecision
   - target: the 9:1 bracket
3. **Everyone holds a veto** — sellers rarely find one person who can approve a deal alone; decision authority rests with a group whose members have different roles and all have veto power [3].
   - sources: [3] Schmidt, Adamson and Bird, Making the Consensus Sale (Harvard Business Review) — https://hbr.org/2015/03/making-the-consensus-sale
   - target: the Opportunity segment
4. **Buying groups disagree** — a Gartner survey of 632 B2B buyers describes buying groups of five to 16 people; 74 percent of teams showed unhealthy conflict, and groups that reached consensus were 2.5 times more likely to report a high-quality deal [4].
   - sources: [4] Gartner, Gartner Sales Survey Finds 74% of B2B Buyer Teams Demonstrate "Unhealthy Conflict" During The Decision Process — https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-07-gartner-sales-survey-finds-74-percent-of-b2b-buyer-teams-demonstrate-unhealthy-conflict-during-the-decision-process
   - target: the Opportunity segment

#### Voice script

1. **Read the picture** — Read the picture. Lead to opportunity is three to one, opportunity to deal is nine to one, so together it takes 27 leads for one deal.
2. **Read the clock** — Now read the clock. Two weeks, six months, three months, which makes 9.5 months from first lead to money. Think one step further and ten deals need 270 leads.
3. **Backward chain** — So chain it backwards. If you want a deal in month X, count back 9.5 months, and by then you need 27 names in the funnel. Do you have 27 names?
4. **Your numbers, not mine** — One thing I want to be straight about: this is my worked example, not a law. Yours will be shorter or longer, which is exactly why you measure your own.

### Why it takes time to ramp up {#why-it-takes-time-to-ramp-up}

#### Visual

master: grid-1x3 · motion: local

Three boxes side by side with oversized numerals: "Preparation" (before: sales deck, pricing, offering, business model; during: demo, meeting slides, proposal; deal: contract, compliance, legal), "Unknown market" (who is really your customer, what are they willing to pay, what problem do they really need solved) and "Unknown metrics" (you don't know conversion rate and sales cycle at the beginning, and you need them for financial backwards planning). Hovering a box grows an accent line above it.

Design reference: reference/source-deck/design-reference.md#page-15

#### Speaker notes

1. **Startups search, they do not execute** — established companies execute a business model, a startup is a temporary organization designed to search for a repeatable and scalable one [1].
   - sources: [1] Steve Blank, The Customer Development Manifesto — https://steveblank.com/category/customer-development-manifesto/
   - target: the "Unknown market" box
2. **Selling blind** — in one case, early orders from industry friends and board connections covered the first months of the revenue plan but did not show why customers would buy, because nobody had run customer discovery [2].
   - sources: [2] Steve Blank, It Must Be A Marketing Problem — https://steveblank.com/2010/02/11/it-must-be-a-marketing-problem/
   - target: the "Unknown market" box
3. **Learn first, codify later** — before product-market fit, rely on personal networks and customer referrals: they do not scale but yield the best opportunities to learn from. Codify the playbook after that learning and prove the unit economics before hiring salespeople aggressively [3].
   - sources: [3] Mark Roberge, The Science of Scaling — https://f.hubspotusercontent10.net/hubfs/6575667/Stage%202s%20Science%20of%20Scaling.pdf
   - target: the "Preparation" box
4. **Metrics lag by one cycle** — a conversion figure for recent leads looks worse than its long-run value because those leads have not yet gone through a full cycle; older results are the better estimate [3].
   - sources: [3] Mark Roberge, The Science of Scaling — https://f.hubspotusercontent10.net/hubfs/6575667/Stage%202s%20Science%20of%20Scaling.pdf
   - target: the "Unknown metrics" box

#### Voice script

1. **Preparation** — The first box is preparation. Before, during and at the deal you need a deck and pricing, a demo and a proposal, a contract and the legal side. None of that exists on day one.
2. **Unknown market** — The second is an unknown market. Who is your customer, what do they pay, which problem really hurts. Only real conversations answer that, and desk research will not.
3. **Unknown metrics** — The third is unknown metrics. You have no conversion rate and no cycle length yet, and you need both to plan backwards from your cash. How many months this takes, I will not invent for you.
4. **So start now** — So start now, because each box shrinks only by doing. How many months do you have left, and how many of these three boxes have you started?
