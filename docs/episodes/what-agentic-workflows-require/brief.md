# What agentic workflows require of people and organisations

## Format

Foundations, 20 to 30 minutes.

## Audience

Team leads, managers and CEOs of smaller companies who use AI chat tools and need to judge what their people and their organisation need to work with agents. It builds on the earlier Episodes (the amnesiac freelancer, managing the context window, from shared understanding to shared context, skills) and points viewers to them rather than starting cold. First shown at the Cyberforum Akademie workshop; reusable in any workshop.

## Style

Board: managers already plan on whiteboards and sticky notes, and planning backwards from a goal and breaking it into tasks is what a board shows.

## Learning goal

Explain what an agentic workflow is and what it requires of each person and of the organisation, so the workflow is designed AI first, around the goal, not around current teams, culture and processes.

## Actionables

1. Pick one recurring process in your team, and mark which steps an agent could run and whether a person stays in, on or out of the loop for each.
2. Before you hand a task to an agent, write down what it receives, what it must deliver, and how someone else can check the result.
3. If you lead a transformation and the AI workflow your people define mirrors how they work today, suspect Conway's law, and redesign it around the goal.
4. Look at your team: who can break work down, write clear handoffs and review an agent's result? Those are the skills to build.

## Hook

"Everyone delivered exactly what was asked, and none of it added up. AI just gets you there faster."

## Red thread

Perfect steps don't add up on their own; an agentic workflow is a goal broken into reviewable handoffs, how far you step out depends on how well you can check them, and whether it works depends on designing it around the goal, not the org chart.

## Analogy

The company summer party for 200 employees across five offices, from the earlier Episodes.

- In the loop: you lead freelancers and approve each step.
- On the loop: your team runs it, and you read reports and step in when needed.
- Out of the loop: an all-inclusive agency delivers it, and you only see the result.
- The party date is the goal you plan backwards from; the venue and the train timetable are external dependencies; the arrival time handed from travel to the programme is a contract at the seam.
- Guests travel from five offices: everyone shares the destination, the arrival time and a travel budget, but nobody prescribes car, train or carpool, which is a contract on the goal, not the process.

### What an agentic workflow is

3. **What an agent is** — the term the workflow builds on: an agent is a language model that uses tools in a loop and decides for itself how to reach a goal (Anthropic, Building effective agents); points viewers back to the earlier Episodes. _Visual:_ the AI capability Explorable with the Agents column lit (a new Preset), its scene playing the agent's loop; the Explorable keeps its own look, a deliberate exception to Board. _Research:_ 2
4. **What an agentic workflow is** — the definition after IBM and Salesforce: structured processes in which AI agents make decisions, take actions and coordinate tasks with minimal human intervention. It works on two levels: you design the path of tasks and handoffs, and the agent steers inside its task. How minimal the intervention is stays open for Slides 5 and 6. _Visual:_ the party board with the goal note on the right and task notes chaining towards it, each arrow labelled with what it hands over and each note marked person or agent; the path is labelled "You design this", and one zoomed agent note "The agent steers this". _Research:_ 3, 4
5. **In, on or out of the loop** — the three levels of human oversight, each defined in one plain line (after Losing Humanity, 2012): in, the agent acts only after a person approves; on, it acts on its own while a person watches and can step in; out, it acts without any person, who sees only the result. Oversight is chosen per task, which answers the "minimal intervention" left open on Slide 4. _Visual:_ the three definitions as a legend beside one party board, each note tagged: guest list _out_, venue _on_, activity _in_. _Research:_ 5
6. **Reviewability sets the distance** — you can only step out of the loop as far as you can check the result, and trust grows with reviewability; making a task checkable is what moves it out. _Visual:_ an axis from in to out with the party tasks placed on it; the activity starts at _in_ (paintball, Slide 1) and slides to _on_ once it gets a check it can pass: everyone can join, wheelchair access, no fitness needed. _Research:_ 6

### What it requires of each person

7. **Plan back from the goal** — backward reasoning, defined first: start from the goal and work back to the steps that must come before it, breaking the goal into subgoals until it reaches where you stand today; state the goal as an outcome, tell dependencies inside your control from those outside it, and find the tasks nobody owned. Resolves Slide 1's last train. _Visual:_ the board read right to left from the goal note "20 June: everyone celebrates together and gets home safely"; working back from "gets home safely" adds a note Slide 1 never had, the shuttle back, and the train timetable is marked as an external dependency. _Research:_ 7, 12
8. **Contracts at the seams** — design by contract, defined first after Meyer: cooperation rests on contracts that state each party's obligations and benefits; agree what each task receives and delivers, not how it is done inside. Resolves Slide 1's 4 p.m. arrival. _Visual:_ the travel → programme seam zoomed, with its contract card — Receives: five offices, venue address, budget per person; Delivers: everyone at the venue by 13:30; Checked by: arrival list against the programme start — and below it "how: car, train or carpool, your choice". _Research:_ 8
9. **Reviewing the result** — verification and validation (Boehm): check the result against its contract, then against the goal; the second check is the human part, and it is what lets you step further out. Resolves Slide 1's paintball. _Visual:_ the paintball note checked twice — against its contract: activity for 200 ✓, within budget ✓, on 20 June ✓; against the goal, "everyone celebrates together": ✗, ten colleagues use wheelchairs. _Research:_ 9

### What it requires of the organisation

10. **The org chart trap** — Conway's law (1968): a workflow people design copies how they communicate and work today; told through the transformation story, unnamed, where a team's draft AI workflow gave one discipline fourteen discovery steps. _Visual:_ the party committee's org chart — ten venue scouts, one caterer, one travel planner — beside its draft workflow, with fourteen venue-scouting steps and one catering step, column heights matching the headcount; a callback to Slide 1's five columns. _Research:_ 10
11. **Designed AI first** — general tips for a lean, AI-native workflow: design from the goal and its measurable outcome, not from today's roles; put a contract on every milestone and leave the inside of each step free; cut seams, merging steps that produce nothing reviewable. Pays off the "get them talking" answer: you didn't need more meetings, you needed contracts. _Visual:_ Slide 10's board redrawn around the goal, with fewer notes and a contract on every arrow, the three tips beside it.
12. **What to look for in your team** — lands the Learning goal and answers Slide 1's question, "What did you need to do?": the skills to build in each person, each tied to the failure it fixed (break the work down from the goal → the last train; write contracts at the seams → the 4 p.m. arrival; review against the contract and the goal → paintball), and what the organisation must provide (clear goals, contracts between teams, a policy on oversight per task, workflows designed around the goal, not the org chart). _Visual:_ Slide 1's question as the heading, and two short columns on the board, one for each person and one for the organisation. _Research:_ 11

## Research questions

1. Why can an AI agent not supply organisational context it was never given? (existing: LLM-0005)
2. Why is an agent defined as a language model that uses tools in a loop and decides for itself how to reach a goal? (existing: LLM-0018)
3. Why do IBM and Salesforce define agentic workflows as structured processes in which AI agents make decisions, take actions and coordinate tasks with minimal human intervention?
4. Why does Anthropic separate workflows, which follow paths fixed in advance, from agents, which steer their own process?
5. Why do human in, on and out of the loop name three distinct levels of oversight? (existing: LLM-0024)
6. Why does how well a result can be checked limit how far human oversight can be reduced? (partial: AIE-0005)
7. Why does backward reasoning, working back from the goal, find the steps and dependencies a plan needs (Pólya, AI planning)?
8. Why does design by contract, agreeing what each party receives and delivers, make handoffs reliable without prescribing how the work is done (Meyer, Applying "Design by Contract", 1992)?
9. Why does checking a result against its specification (verification) not show whether it serves the goal (validation), after Boehm?
10. Why do the workflows an organisation designs copy its communication structure, as Conway's law states for systems "defined broadly"? (partial: SWE-0001)
11. Why are breaking work down, writing handoffs and reviewing results the skills people need to work with agents? (partial: AIE-0006, AIE-0018)
12. Why do language models plan worse backwards than forwards (arXiv 2411.01790)?
