# Why do people remember the start, the end and the standout moments of a long conversation?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that a person recalls a long sequence of events unevenly: the opening, the close and a few distinctive moments survive, while the middle blurs. The question is scoped to the memory effects themselves, the serial position effect and the isolation effect, not to any particular medium.

## Answer

People recall the first items (primacy) and the last items (recency) of a sequence better than the middle, so recall over position is U-shaped. Early items are rehearsed more and so reach longer-term memory, while the latest items are still held in short-term memory when recall begins. An item that stands out from its neighbours is remembered better than the surrounding ones (the isolation effect). The mechanisms are debated: recency also appears over long delays, which a pure short-term-store account cannot explain, and the data come from lab word lists, so applying them to a free-flowing conversation is an inference.

## Findings

1. In free recall of a word list, the probability of recall is highest for the first and the last items and lowest for the middle ones [1].
2. Delaying recall with a distractor task removes the recency part of the curve but leaves primacy intact, while slowing the presentation rate improves primacy but not recency; this supports two separate stores, a short-term one for recency and a long-term one for primacy [2].
3. Participants who rehearse aloud rehearse early items more often than middle items, and the number of rehearsals predicts the recall of those items, which accounts for primacy [3].
4. Recency does not need a short-term store alone: recency also appears when recall is delayed across a long series of items, because the most recent items are the easiest to retrieve from long-term memory [4].
5. An item isolated within a homogeneous series is reproduced better than an item in a uniform series, the result that gave the isolation effect its name [5]. Distinctiveness is relative: an item stands out against the similarity of its context, not through its own properties alone [6].

## Caveats

All six sources use word lists or similar short material in a lab, not a spoken conversation of an hour or more. That the same pattern holds for a long phone call is an inference, though the primacy, recency and isolation effects are standard textbook findings. The origin of recency is disputed between the two-store account [2] and the retrieval account [4]; the Answer reports both rather than choosing one.
