# Why does selling early build customer trust beyond the revenue itself?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that starting to sell before revenue is needed earns something a later start cannot: a customer's trust in a seller. The claim concerns what repeated seller-buyer contact and early commitments do to trust, and when they fail to.

## Answer

Early selling is likely to build trust when it gives a buyer reason to expect continued dealings and the seller backs that with costly, specific commitments. The evidence supports prior history only indirectly: one study finds that history raises trust only through those continuity expectations, and with weak expectations a longer history lowers trust. Outreach alone does not guarantee trust: in the strongest counter-evidence on selection, trust did not decide the current supplier choice once prior experience and performance were controlled. Trust is more clearly tied to future purchase intention, and relationship marketing works better when built with an individual person. That the trust payoff applies to a startup's early outreach is an inference (see Caveats).

## Findings

1. In a survey of more than 200 purchasing managers, several variables influenced trust of a supplier firm and of its salesperson, and that trust influenced the buyer's anticipated future interaction with the supplier [^doney-cannon-trust].
2. The same study is the strongest counter-evidence: after controlling for previous experience and supplier performance, neither trust of the selling firm nor of its salesperson influenced the current supplier selection decision [^doney-cannon-trust]. Salesperson trust acted only indirectly, through supplier-firm trust. Trust built early therefore shows up in later dealings, not necessarily in the first purchase.
3. Across alliances made between 1970 and 1989, repeated alliances between the same two partners were less likely than other alliances to be organised using equity, which the author reads as evidence of trust emerging from repeated ties; trust was inferred from governance choice, not measured directly [^gulati-familiarity].
4. This prior-history explanation is disputed. Poppo, Zhou and Ryu found that prior history does not directly affect trust: the positive relationship is mediated by expectations of continuity, and a longer history strengthens the effect of continuity on trust. Unexpectedly, after specifying that path, prior history had a direct negative effect on trust, and when expectations of continuity were weak, trust was lower for exchanges with a longer prior history [^poppo-zhou-ryu-origins]. This is the strongest counter-evidence to Gulati: history alone is not enough, and early contact that gives no reason to expect a continuing relationship may not build trust.
5. In 378 manufacturer-distributor pairs, idiosyncratic investments, a kind of pledge, had a strong effect on both parties' commitment, and each party's commitment was affected by the other's perceived commitment. The study measured commitment, not trust [^anderson-weitz-pledges].
6. Relationship marketing theory holds that successful relational exchange requires relationship commitment and trust as key mediating variables, and a test on automobile tire retailers favoured that model over a rival without them [^morgan-hunt-commitment-trust].
7. A meta-analysis finds that relationship marketing in general positively affects seller performance, and that it is more effective when relationships matter more to customers (services, channel exchanges, business markets) and when they are built with an individual person rather than a selling firm. The finding concerns relationship marketing outcomes, not trust specifically [^palmatier-meta-analysis].

## Caveats

The Sources were read as publisher and indexed abstracts; the full texts are paywalled and were not read, so effect sizes and sample details are not reported here. All are business-to-business studies of purchasing managers, distributors, alliance partners and tire retailers, not of early-stage startups selling to first customers, so applying them to a startup is an inference, as is the Answer's step from these associations to "early selling builds trust". Gulati inferred trust from alliance governance rather than measuring it, and is disputed by Poppo, Zhou and Ryu; Anderson and Weitz measured commitment, not trust, so linking pledges to trust is an inference; Palmatier et al. concern relationship marketing effectiveness generally, and its individual-person moderator is not shown for trust. Doney and Cannon found salesperson trust acts only indirectly through supplier-firm trust. Listening to customers as a trust mechanism is not tested in these Sources and is not part of the Answer. The studies show associations in their samples and cannot show that outreach by itself creates trust.
