# Why does building an effective sales pipeline take months to ramp up?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that a sales pipeline should be started well before its revenue is needed. The claim needs the delay explained by its separate causes, because each cause responds to different actions, so the question is scoped to why a pipeline is slow to become productive, not to how long it takes.

## Answer

Ramp-up is best understood as three distinct lags rather than one, a separation the sources support only conceptually. Pipeline construction is the work of finding, qualifying and converting prospects into opportunities. Seller ramp time is how long a new seller needs before the activity becomes productive. Buyer sales-cycle lag is the time buyers take to decide, which a seller can influence but not set. The sources support the existence and separation of these lags, not a universal number of months; any duration depends on the offering, the buyer, the seller and the stage of the company.

## Findings

1. Pipeline construction: in a study of 200 generalist salespeople with matched performance data, heavy prospecting effort could lower the value of a salesperson's accounts when it came at the expense of closing, retaining and growing business. Prospecting became a powerful tool when it led to better conversion opportunities, so a pipeline needs both leads and the conversion work that turns them into opportunities [^peasley-hochstein-early-tenure].
2. Pipeline construction at a start-up: a longitudinal survey of founders of B2B high-tech start-ups describes the early stage as one of severe challenges in establishing customer relationships for unfamiliar, innovative and complex offerings. Start-ups that put a larger share of their budget into personal sales performed better in the early life-cycle stages, and worse in later ones [^vomberg-b2b-startup-budgets].
3. Seller ramp time: the authors of the early-tenure study frame the ramp-up period as the transition from onboarding to the sales role, typically spanning the first 18-24 months after hire. That span is their framing, not a measured ramp duration: they surveyed 221 generalist salespeople hired 18-24 months before the study. Which activities these salespeople concentrated on affected year-over-year sales growth by the end of the first full year [^peasley-hochstein-early-tenure].
4. Seller ramp time, experience and training: in a structural model fitted to one firm's sales force, tenure had a positive effect on performance, and training improved performance more for junior than for senior salespeople. New hires in the model start with zero tenure and no training and perform below established salespeople [^chung-sales-force-management].
5. Buyer sales-cycle lag: interviews with B2B executives and salespeople describe business customers as increasingly technology-empowered, following buyer-led purchase journeys. The salesperson's role shifts toward orchestrating all buyer-seller touchpoints along that journey, so pacing sits with the buyer's own process and not only with the seller's effort [^kalwey-holistic-selling].

## Caveats

No source gives a duration for building a pipeline from nothing, and none should be read as one. The 18-24 months is the studied salespeople's tenure at survey time and the authors' typical framing of the ramp-up period; the study did not measure how long ramp-up lasts, and the sample is one firm with a shared training curriculum, compensation structure and culture. It does not transfer to a founder, a different product or a different market. The Peasley and Hochstein finding is cited through the authors' own summary because the journal article is paywalled. The Chung study models one firm's sales force and shows the direction of the tenure and training effects, not a ramp length. The Vomberg study concerns B2B high-tech start-ups, and its finding on personal-sales budgets is about firm performance, not pipeline timing. Kalwey et al. is a conceptual paper built on practitioner interviews; it does not measure sales-cycle length. How long buyers take is therefore supported here only as a separate, buyer-led lag, and the causes of its length belong to the research on B2B sales cycles. Treating the three lags as separate, and as overlapping in practice, is a conceptual inference: no Finding demonstrates their overlap or any combined duration.
