# Why do typical B2B sales cycles take as long as they do?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that a business-to-business deal closes months after first contact, so a seller must start before the revenue is needed. The question is about the causes of that elapsed time, not about a typical number of months.

## Answer

A B2B purchase is usually decided by a group, not a person: several people across functions must agree, and disagreement among them is common, which stretches the time to a decision. Buyers that are public bodies add legally mandated minimum waiting periods on top. How long a cycle runs therefore varies with who buys, what is bought and how the buyer is organised, so no single length, such as an illustrative 9.5 months, is universal. The sources here support the causes, not a benchmark duration.

## Findings

1. A Gartner survey of 632 B2B buyers (August to September 2024) found buying groups ranging from five to 16 people across as many as four functions, each member with possibly differing priorities and opinions [^gartner-buyer-conflict-2025].
2. In the same survey, 74% of buyer teams showed "unhealthy conflict": members had conflicting objectives, disagreed on the best course of action, or were overruled by external decision-makers. Buying groups that reached consensus were 2.5 times more likely to report a high-quality deal [^gartner-buyer-conflict-2025].
3. An early study of capital-equipment and industrial-service purchases in 31 firms treated the buying task group, the buying center, as a network of people whose composition and interaction can be measured. Equipment and service purchases differed reliably on those measures, and organisational structure and purchase situation correlated with the dimensions of the group, so group shape varies by what is bought and who buys [^johnston-bonoma-buying-center].
4. Public buyers in the EU must, in an open procedure, give at least 35 days from the date the contract notice was sent for tenders to be received. That ordinary minimum can be shortened: to 15 days where a qualifying prior information notice was published 35 days to 12 months earlier, to not less than 15 days where the buyer duly substantiates a state of urgency, and by five days where it accepts electronic submission [^eu-directive-2014-24].
5. US federal agencies must publish a notice at least 15 days before issuing a solicitation, allow at least 30 days from issuance of a solicitation for bids or proposals above the simplified acquisition threshold, and allow at least 45 days from publication of the notice for research and development actions above it. For acquisitions covered by the WTO Government Procurement Agreement or a free trade agreement, at least 40 days must pass between publication of the notice and receipt of offers, reducible to as few as 10 where an annual forecast was published. Rules for commercial products and services and for smaller amounts differ [^far-5-203].

## Caveats

- No source here measures the length of a B2B sales cycle, so none supports a typical duration or the illustrative 9.5 months; cycle length depends on deal size, product, buyer organisation and whether the buyer is public.
- The Gartner figures come from one vendor's survey of buyers, reported in a press release without the full method; group sizes of five to 16 are a range for that sample, not a rule for every deal, and small purchases may be decided by one person.
- Johnston and Bonoma is a 1981 study of 31 firms and capital purchases; it shows variation in group shape, not elapsed time.
- The procurement rules show only minimum legal waiting times for public buyers. That risk checks, budget approval and security or legal review in private companies lengthen cycles is an inference; the sources here do not measure them, and the Answer's link from group disagreement to a longer decision is an inference too, since the sources report conflict and consensus, not duration.
