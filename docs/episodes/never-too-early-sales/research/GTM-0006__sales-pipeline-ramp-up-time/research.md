# Why does building an effective sales pipeline take months to ramp up?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that a sales pipeline cannot be switched on when money is needed, because it has to be built and tuned over time. The question is about the startup case, where the market, the buyer and the sales metrics are not yet known.

## Answer

A startup's pipeline takes months to ramp up because a startup is still searching for a business model, not executing a known one. Founders have to get customers by hand, learn who buys and why, and write that down as a repeatable process before more sales effort pays off. Pipeline metrics such as conversion also lag, since a lead counts only after it has passed through a full sales cycle. Early orders from friends and contacts do not prove the process works.

The ramp-up has three parts, each a reason it cannot be rushed. First, preparation: the sales process has to be found and written down before it can be repeated. Second, an unknown market: who buys, why and at what price is learned only through real conversations. Third, unknown metrics: conversion and sales cycle can be measured only after leads have passed through a full cycle.

## Findings

1. Established companies execute a business model, while startups search for one; a startup is defined as a temporary organization designed to search for a repeatable and scalable business model [^blank-manifesto].
2. A business plan does not survive first contact with customers, so the facts a pipeline depends on (who buys, why, at what price) are found outside the building, in conversations with customers [^blank-manifesto].
3. In Blank's account of a startup that missed its sales plan, early orders from industry friends and board connections covered the first months of the revenue plan but did not show why customers would buy. Nobody had run Customer Discovery, so sales were "selling blind" [^blank-marketing-problem].
4. In the same account, no one knew what a repeatable sales model looked like before the sales force was scaled. Reps improvised their own pitches while cash flowed out. Blank concludes that premature scaling of sales and marketing is the leading cause of cash loss in a startup, and that scaling should follow the founders finding a repeatable sales model [^blank-marketing-problem].
5. Customers do not arrive on their own: "You can't wait for users to come to you. You have to go out and get them." Getting users is a gradual process, and early numbers look too small only to those who underestimate compound growth [^graham-do-things-that-dont-scale].
6. A founder's initial model of users is always inaccurate, even if the founder is one of them, and direct contact with the earliest users gives feedback that stays the most valuable [^graham-do-things-that-dont-scale].
7. At the stage before product-market fit, Roberge advises relying on personal networks of founders, employees and investors and on customer referrals. These channels do not scale but yield the best opportunities to learn from; the goal there is maximum learning, not profit [^roberge-science-of-scaling].
8. Roberge advises codifying the go-to-market playbook (buyer journey, prospecting, discovery and presentation guides) only after that learning, and proving it yields scalable unit economics before aggressively hiring salespeople [^roberge-science-of-scaling].
9. Pipeline metrics lag by the sales cycle: a conversion figure for recent leads looks worse than the long-run value because those leads have not yet gone through a full cycle, so older results are the better estimate [^roberge-science-of-scaling].
10. Roberge's unit-economics model leaves out sales cycle and salesperson ramp time, and says their effect on cash flow must be modelled separately [^roberge-science-of-scaling].

## Caveats

The Sources are practitioner accounts from investors and operators, not controlled studies, so they show the mechanism more than a measured duration. They give no benchmark for how many months a ramp-up takes; that depends on price, buyer and cycle length, and was not found in a primary source. The Answer's grouping into three parts (preparation, unknown market, unknown metrics) is this file's own synthesis of Findings 3 to 9, not a framing any Source states, which makes it an inference. Blank's account is a single case. The question on why B2B sales cycles are long, and the buying-group evidence for it, belongs to a separate research file.
