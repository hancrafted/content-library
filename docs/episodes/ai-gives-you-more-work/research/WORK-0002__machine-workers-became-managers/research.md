# Why did workers who took up managing new machines, like the steam engine, become managers while others stayed workers?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that when a machine took over predictable work, the people who learned to manage it moved up into management while those who stayed on the line remained workers. The question tests that claim against economic history: who the early industrial managers were, and how they were recruited.

## Answer

The premise holds in part and is overstated as a general rule. Some skilled workmen did advance to overlooker, manager or partner, and a few skilled mechanics and engineers became hired managers. But the sources found show no single route into early management: owners had difficulty finding managers, formal training was almost absent, and in the best-documented cases managers were hired by family firms or came with skills from other mills. No source found measures how many managers came from the shop floor, so "operators of new machines became the managers" cannot be stated as a finding.

## Findings

1. Andrew Ure, a contemporary defender of the factory system, wrote that workers who recommended themselves by regularity and skill to monied men "would have advanced their condition to that of overlookers, managers, and partners in new mills" [1].
2. In a sample of British mechanics and engineers of the Industrial Revolution, "385 (51 percent of our sample and 64 percent of all those whose means of livelihood could be established) were identifiable entrepreneurs and independent operators or consultants", and "A respectable 18 percent were hired engineers and managers" [2].
3. The same study reports that "Two thirds of those whose educational background could be established were apprenticed" [2].
4. A British official told a Parliamentary Committee in 1840 that in the cotton mills in the Vienna area "the directors and foremen are chiefly Englishmen or Scotsmen from the cotton manufactories of Glasgow and Manchester" [2].
5. Murphy, discussing Sidney Pollard's The Genesis of Modern Management, quotes Pollard that "formal management training was so rare as to be negligible", and that many eighteenth-century entrepreneurs, like James Watt, had "excessive difficulty in finding intelligent managing clerks" [3].
6. Murphy also shows a clerk at the Bank of England rising to chief clerk by learning each office on the job at the Bank's expense, which she presents as expertise gained on the job rather than through external training; she adds that detailed knowledge of working practices "is not the same as managerial skill" [3].
7. Tucker finds that early textile firms were reluctant to hand "family-dominated, entrepreneurial functions to hired factory agents", and that Samuel Slater gave wide authority in individual mills to "what is perhaps the earliest example of the professional manager" only when he and his family could not manage a fast-growing, shifting industry in the 1820s and 1830s [4].
8. Bessen estimates that human capital investments in mule spinners and power loom tenders in ante-bellum Lowell "rivaled those of craft apprentices", so the workers who stayed on the machines were not unskilled [5].

## Caveats

- Pollard's book itself was not read: it is not freely available, and Findings 5 relies on Murphy quoting it. Pollard's own account of where managers came from (owners' relatives, promoted workmen, clerks, merchants) is not checked here, so a reader should open the chapter "The Managers" before claiming a breakdown.
- Finding 1 is Ure's claim, a partisan 1835 argument that workers who behaved well could rise; it is not evidence of how many did.
- Finding 2 describes tweakers (mechanics and engineers), a top few percent of the labour force, not ordinary machine operators; the share who became managers (18 percent hired engineers and managers) is for that sample only, and the study notes textiles differ from other sectors.
- Findings 6 and 7 come from a bank and an American mill, not the steam engine; they show routes into management, not that steam-engine users took them.
- The Answer's verdict that the premise is "overstated" is an inference from the absence of evidence that machine operators were the main route, together with evidence of other routes. Absence from these sources is not proof of absence.
- The sources do not explain why some workers advanced and others did not, so the question's "why" is answered only as far as recruitment routes and skill, not individual selection.
