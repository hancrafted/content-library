# Why did new technologies like the steam engine change social mobility for the workers who adopted them?

## Context

Asked to support a claim about whether people who worked alongside a new machine moved up in occupation and status because of it. The evidence sits in economic history: studies of skills, wages and schooling in industrialising regions, and studies of occupational mobility between fathers and sons.

## Answer

The evidence supports a narrower claim than the question assumes. Where steam engines were used more, English counties had a larger share of skilled workers [1], so the technology did shift the skill mix of a region. No source found here follows individual workers who adopted a new machine and shows them moving up as a result. The mobility studies measure fathers and sons across whole economies, not machine adopters, and their conclusions are disputed [5][6][7][8]. Wage, schooling and child-labour evidence for the same period is mixed to pessimistic [1][3][4], so "adopters rose" is overstated as a general rule.

## Findings

1. Using the number of steam engines installed by 1800 and occupational statistics by county, the authors "establish a positive correlation between the use of steam engines and the share of skilled workers at the county level", and use coal-bearing rock strata to show "that the effect was causal" [1].
2. The same study reports that "While technological change stimulated the formation of working skills, it had an overall negative effect on the formation of primary education, captured by literacy and school enrolment rates. It also led to higher gender inequality in literacy." [1]
3. At the Lowell textile mills in 1842, firms "increased weaving productivity by assigning three looms per worker instead of two", and "Before, weavers at Lowell were temporary and mostly literate Yankee farm girls; afterwards, firms increasingly hired local residents, including illiterate and Irish workers." [2]
4. Autobiographies of working men show "an upsurge in child labour in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries with children's work entrenched in traditional sectors as well as spreading in newly mechanized factories and workshops", linked to "an increase in the relative productivity of children as a result of mechanization" [3].
5. New estimates of real earnings find "that the standard of living of the average working-class family improved by less than 15 percent between the 1780s and 1850s" [4].
6. On occupational mobility between fathers and sons, Long and Ferrie report that "The U.S. was substantially more mobile then Britain through 1900" and that "The margin by which U.S. mobility exceeded British mobility was erased by the 1950s" [5]. The study compares two countries and is not about machine adopters.
7. The strongest counter-evidence to that finding: Xie and Killewald write "We show that Long and Ferrie's main conclusion is all driven by this misleading result for farmers" [6]. Hout and Guest "reanalyze Long and Ferrie's data" and "find that the association of occupational status across generations was quite similar over time and place" [7].
8. Long and Ferrie's reply: "We do not dispute Guest and Hout's characterization of the importance of total mobility in addition to relative mobility. We find much in their additional analyses that supports our original findings." [8]

## Caveats

- Inference in the Answer: that no study follows individual adopters is a statement about what this search found, not a proven absence. Searches for the occupational-mobility literature by David Mitch and Gregory Clark returned no source whose abstract could be quoted here, so their work is not used.
- Inference in the Answer: that wage, schooling and child-labour results make "adopters rose" overstated joins findings about regions and populations [1][3][4] to a claim about workers who adopted a machine; none of those sources isolates adopters.
- Finding 1 shows a county-level correlation between engines and skilled-worker share; it does not show that the workers using engines were the ones counted as skilled, or that they or their children moved up.
- Findings 3 and 4 concern one firm's staffing and a set of autobiographies; they do not generalise to every adopting workforce.
- Findings 6 to 8 are a live dispute about how to measure mobility, and sit on father-son comparisons between countries, so they bear on social mobility in industrialising economies and not on steam adoption specifically.
- Abstracts were read for all eight sources; full texts were not checked for the findings beyond the abstract wording.
