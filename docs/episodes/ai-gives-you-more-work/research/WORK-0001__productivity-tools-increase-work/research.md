# Why do productivity tools like the plough, the steam engine and the computer increase total work instead of reducing it?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that a tool taking over part of a task does not shrink the total work: the saved effort is reinvested in more output, more ambitious output or the tasks the tool leaves to people. The question is scoped to the economic mechanism, not to any one technology.

## Answer

A tool that makes each unit of output cheaper rarely lowers the total work, because demand for the output grows faster than the labour per unit falls. The steam engine's efficiency raised coal consumption instead of lowering it, and cotton, steel and car employment grew for a century or more while productivity rose. The tool also raises the value of the tasks it leaves to people, which is why bank tellers survived the ATM. The effect is not universal: it lasts while demand is elastic, and the plough and household appliances are the weaker, partly disputed cases.

## Findings

1. Jevons wrote that "it is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption", because new modes of economy lead to an increase of consumption [1].
2. For the steam engine, Jevons argued that Watt's inventions were modes of economising heat and that further improvement would only accelerate coal consumption. In Scotland, coal use per ton of iron fell to under a third of its former amount while total consumption rose about tenfold [1].
3. In US cotton textiles, steel and automobiles, automation first spurred job growth because demand was highly elastic. Demand later became satiated and employment fell, an inverted U [3].
4. Output per hour in cotton textiles rose over 800-fold from 1810 to 2011, against a 30-fold rise in real GDP per capita while employment in the industry grew for decades [3].
5. Automation substitutes for some tasks but complements labour, raises output and raises the value of the remaining human tasks [2].
6. Bank teller employment rose from about 500,000 to about 550,000 between 1980 and 2010 as ATMs quadrupled. Tellers per branch fell by more than a third while branches rose by more than 40 percent, and tellers shifted to relationship banking [2].
7. Over the very long run gains in productivity have not led to a shortfall of demand: an average worker in 2015 could match the 1915 income by working about 17 weeks a year, but few would choose that, so consumption demands rose with productivity [2].
8. Autor also notes that a sector can shrink as productivity rises, as food did, but the surplus income is spent elsewhere, so aggregate demand does not fall [2].
9. For the plough, Boserup argues that where population pressure forces shorter fallow, the plough is "a means to prevent a fall in output per man-hour rather than a means to raise it" [4].
10. Boserup shows that cropping land more often raises labour per hectare: irrigated Chinese cereals can take up to 600 working days per crop hectare, ten to twenty times the labour of extensive dry plough cultivation [4].
11. For household technology, Ruth Schwartz Cowan argued that appliances reduced drudgery but not the time spent, because they let one housewife meet a middle-class standard of health and cleanliness that once needed a staff of three or four. Ramey reports this argument, called the "Cowan Paradox" by Mokyr [5].
12. Ramey's re-estimate from time diaries disputes the no-decline view: housewives' home-production time fell by about six hours a week from 1900 to 1965, and per capita time across the whole population rose only slightly over the century [5].

## Caveats

Boserup's plough finding concerns population pressure and intensification, so the plough is not itself the cause of added work, and her conclusion rests on a book-length argument rather than a controlled test. The housework case is disputed: Ramey finds no robust "Cowan Paradox" for housewives, so it should not be presented as settled. Bessen's elasticity argument shows that employment falls once demand saturates. The step from "more total work" to "more management work" is inference; none of these sources measure management time. Cowan's own text was not read, only Ramey's quotation of it.
