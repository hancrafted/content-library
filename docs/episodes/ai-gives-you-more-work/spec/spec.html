Why AI gives you more work, and why you'll want it

Each Slide shows its words as tabs above its Options, which are tabs too: open each, pick one, and dictate a note where it should change. Save at the bottom when done.

Brief

Learning goal

Explain why AI adds work rather than removing it, and name the management skills that enable you to do more complex tasks with it.

Actionables

  • Next time you hand work to a colleague, give them a proper handoff with enough context.
  • When you onboard a new team member, treat it like opening a new AI session.
  • When you get a complex project, break it into smaller chunks and define the seams between them, so each task contributes to the overall goal.

Research questions

  • Why do productivity tools like the plough, the steam engine and the computer increase total work instead of reducing it?
  • Why does AI let individuals take on more complex tasks than before?
  • Why do delegation and management skills improve results when working with AI?
  • Why does Matt Pocock describe the skills for working with AI as a language barrier?
  • Why do language models, unlike earlier machines and tools, produce non-deterministic results?
  • Why do people working with AI keep executing the work themselves instead of managing it?
  • Why did workers who took up managing new machines, like the steam engine, become managers while others stayed workers?
  • Why did new technologies like the steam engine change social mobility for the workers who adopted them?

Section 1 · section slide, drawn in the Title-slide Options

From execution to management

  1. The question — Imagine a colleague asks you over coffee: will AI reduce my work? Will it speed things up, so that by next year I do less? It is a fair question, and almost everybody starting out with AI asks it.
  2. The reframe — But the question rests on a faulty premise, because it assumes the tasks stay the same size. They don't. With AI you can now take on far more complex tasks than you could alone, and managing those tasks is the work AI adds.
  3. The payoff — So the better question is what you can now do. You accomplish much more, if you are willing to upskill and learn to manage AI like a team, and today is the chance to start moving in that direction.

Every revolution, one card

with every innovation, a machine took over predictable work and people moved up to the unpredictable parts: judging, deciding, coordinating. Those who took up managing the machines became the managers, while those who stayed on the lines stayed workers, nothing wrong with that; with a seismic shift like AI, there is this potential for people to become the new managers. AI is the first tool that does part of that unpredictable work itself (natural language, reasoning, creativity, brainstorming), so you move up again, now managing something that thinks; one person can do tasks that used to need a whole team

Visual idea

master: grid-bento · motion: local

A row of cards for farming, the steam engine and the computer, followed by a much larger AI card as the lead tile. Each card has two layers: on top what the machine took over, below where people moved up (the plough digs, the farmer plans the harvest; the engine does the lifting, the worker plans the line; AI drafts and reasons, you manage it like a team). At rest only the top layers show. Progressive disclosure reveals the lower layer card by card, so the climb builds, and the AI card lands last as the biggest step. Under the steam engine card sits a small group of workers who upskilled to manage the plant and now live a different life, while the others stay factory workers, drawn with no judgment. The AI card repeats the fork, left open.

Voice script

  1. Every revolution, one card — To see why, look at the three machines you already know: the plough, the steam engine and the computer. Each one took over the predictable work, the lifting, the digging, the calculating, and people moved to the part a machine could not do, which is judging, deciding and coordinating.
  2. The climb — Now watch what happens under each card. The plough digs, so the farmer plans the harvest. The engine does the lifting, so the worker plans the line. Every time, the machine did more, and you moved up a level, which means the work did not disappear, it changed.
  3. The fork, no judgment — Here is where it forks, and I want to say it without judgment. Picture two people in the same plant on the same Monday, one still on the line and one at the control desk, and both are doing honest work. What matters for you is that a seismic shift opens that door, and whether you step through it is your call.
  4. Now it thinks — What is different now is that AI is the first tool that does part of the unpredictable work itself. It works in natural language, it reasons, it is creative, it brainstorms, so for the first time you are managing something that thinks.
  5. Biggest step — That is the biggest step on the wall, because one person can now do tasks that used to need a whole team. The fork under the AI card is still open, and this next part is my hypothesis: it opens again, for anyone who wants to step through it.

Speaker notes

  1. A cheaper unit does not mean less total work — Jevons argued Watt's inventions economised heat and that further improvement would accelerate coal use; separately, in Scotland coal per ton of iron fell to under a third while total consumption rose about tenfold [1].
    • [1] Jevons, The Coal Question (1865) — https://www.econlib.org/library/YPDBooks/Jevons/jvnCQ.html
  2. The tool lifts the tasks left to people — when ATMs quadrupled, tellers per branch fell by more than a third, yet branches rose by more than 40 percent and tellers moved towards relationship banking [2].
    • [2] Autor, Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? The History and Future of Workplace Automation — https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.29.3.3
    • target: the lower layer of the steam engine and computer cards
  3. The plough is the weaker card — Boserup finds the plough often prevented a fall in output per man-hour rather than raised it, and the extra work came from cropping land more often [4].
    • [4] Boserup, The Conditions of Agricultural Growth — https://www.biw.kuleuven.be/aee/clo/idessa_files/boserup1965.pdf
    • target: the farming card
  4. Some workers did rise, by several routes — a contemporary defender of the factory system claimed regular, skilled workers could advance to overlooker, manager or partner, and 18 percent of a sample of British mechanics and engineers became hired engineers and managers; but owners found managers by many routes, and no source measures how many came from the shop floor [1][2].
    • [1] Ure, The Philosophy of Manufactures (1835) — https://archive.org/details/philosophyofmanu00urea
    • [2] Meisenzahl and Mokyr, The Rate and Direction of Invention in the British Industrial Revolution: Incentives and Institutions — https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16993/w16993.pdf
    • target: the fork under the steam engine card
  5. Steam shifted a region's skill mix — English counties that used more steam engines had a larger share of skilled workers; no study found follows individual adopters rising, and schooling and wage evidence for the period is mixed [1].
    • [1] de Pleijt, Nuvolari and Weisdorf, Human Capital Formation During the First Industrial Revolution: Evidence from the use of Steam Engines — https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvz006
    • target: the fork under the steam engine card
  6. AI completes longer tasks on its own — the task length a frontier model completes at 50 percent success was about 50 minutes in early 2025 and has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019 [1].
    • [1] Kwa et al., Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks (METR) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499
    • target: the AI card
  7. The gain is real but conditional — 758 BCG consultants using AI finished 12.2 percent more tasks, 25.1 percent faster, at over 40 percent higher quality inside the model's frontier, and were 19 percentage points less likely to be correct on a task outside it; a trial with 16 experienced developers found a 19 percent slowdown with early-2025 tools [3][5].
    • [3] Dell'Acqua et al., Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier, HBS Working Paper 24-013 (SSRN 4573321) — https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf
    • [5] Becker, Rush, Barnes, Rein, Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity — https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089
    • target: the AI card
  8. The same prompt can answer differently — across five models and eight tasks run ten times with settings meant to be deterministic, accuracy varied by up to 15 percent between runs, unlike a plough or a steam engine [3].
    • [3] Atil et al., LLM Stability: A detailed analysis with some surprises — https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667
    • target: the AI card

You were promoted

AI promotes you from execution into management, and that misalignment is the biggest one people face today: most still act as if they do the work rather than manage it. The skills to cross it are management skills, which Matt Pocock calls the language barrier

Visual idea

master: horizontal-rail · motion: local

A person standing in an "execution" box on the left and a "management" box on the right, with the gap between them labelled "the misalignment". Progressive disclosure fills the gap with four stepping stones in order (break it down, write clear goals, review, delegate); the last reveal names the gap the language barrier.

Voice script

  1. You were promoted — So here is the way to read it. AI promotes you. You move from doing the work to managing the work, and the work doesn't shrink, it changes, so that you can do things that were out of reach before.
  2. The misalignment — That move has a gap, and it is the biggest misalignment people face today. Many of us still sit in the execution box, acting as if we do the work ourselves, when the new job is to manage it.
  3. Four stepping stones — The way across is four stepping stones: break it down, write clear goals, review, and delegate. Notice that none of these is a technical skill, you already know them from working with people, and each one makes you more confident with what comes back.
  4. The barrier — And there is a reason the gap is real. Picture asking the AI for "the usual report" and getting back something polished that answers a question you never asked. Nothing broke, it just had none of the words in your head. So before your next handoff, spend one minute writing down the terms you take for granted, and use them every time.

Speaker notes

  1. Many skip the management steps — in 9,830 Claude.ai conversations, 51.1 percent stated a goal, 15.8 percent questioned the reasoning and 8.7 percent checked facts; no study measures executing versus managing, so the research supports "many", not "most" [1].
    • [1] Anthropic, The AI Fluency Index (February 2026) — https://academy.claude.com/tutorials/the-ai-fluency-index
    • target: the gap labelled the misalignment
  2. Workers describe the shift themselves — in a survey of 319 weekly GenAI users, effort moved from executing tasks to stewarding them and from gathering information to verifying it, while higher confidence in the AI went with less checking [5].
    • [5] Lee et al., The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking: Self-Reported Reductions in Cognitive Effort and Confidence Effects From a Survey of Knowledge Workers (CHI 2025) — https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf
  3. Stepping stone: break it down — users split complex tasks into smaller steps, which creates a feedback loop where the model corrects course [2].
    • [2] Anthropic, Economic Index: New building blocks for AI use (January 2026) — https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-primitives
    • target: the first stepping stone
  4. Pocock's words are "communication barrier" — he says there is a communication barrier between a person and an AI agent, located in vocabulary: they do not share the same language, so you need a shared language; the phrase "language barrier" is not his, and the management reading is this project's inference [1].
    • [1] Matt Pocock, Software Fundamentals Matter More Than Ever (AI Engineer Europe 2026) — https://ai.engineer/talks/v4F1gFy-hqg-software-fundamentals-for-ai-coding
    • target: the last reveal

Practise it today, at work

these skills can be practised now with colleagues, so people skills become AI skills; more complex projects are now within reach, an invitation to grow, not a burden

Visual idea

master: grid-3x1 · motion: local

Three rows, each pairing a moment at work with its AI equivalent. At rest only the work side shows; each click reveals the AI side. Row one: hand off work to a colleague, or before a vacation, with a proper handoff and enough context, mirrors starting a new chat with that context. Row two: onboard a new team member mirrors opening a new AI session. Row three: break down a big project into chunks, with the seams between them defined so each task feeds the overall goal, mirrors splitting work into AI tasks. The closing line "more complex projects are now within reach" sits under the rows.

Voice script

  1. Practise on people — The good news is that you don't have to wait for a new tool to practise this. You can practise on people, today, at work, so the people skills you already have become your AI skills.
  2. The handoff — Take the handoff. Next time you hand work to a colleague, before a vacation or any other time, give them a proper handoff with enough context to carry on without you. That is the same move as starting a new chat with the context in it.
  3. The new teammate — Or take the new teammate. When you onboard someone, treat it like opening a new AI session: nothing carries over unless you say it, so tell them what you would tell the AI.
  4. Chunks and seams — Then the big project. When you get a complex one, break it into smaller chunks and define the seams between them, so that each task contributes to the overall goal. Do that, and you can hand the chunks to people or to AI, and stay prolific without losing the thread.
  5. An invitation — Which brings it back to our coffee question. More complex projects are now within reach, which is not a burden, it is an invitation to grow, so which of these three rows will you start with this week?

Speaker notes

  1. Briefing, context and checking are the discipline — Dell'Acqua [3] finds AI helps less experienced people on bounded tasks inside the model's competence; that effective delegation rests on briefing, supplying context and verifying is this project's inference from the research file, not a finding of the study.
    • [3] Dell'Acqua et al., Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier, HBS Working Paper 24-013, September 2023 (SSRN 4573321) — https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf
    • target: all three rows
  2. A new AI session is a new contributor — experienced developers described AI as a new contributor lacking tacit knowledge of the repository, which is hard to hand over as context, and spent about 9 percent of their time reviewing and cleaning its output [7].
    • [7] Becker, Rush, Barnes, Rein, Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity — https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089
    • target: the onboarding row
  3. Experience with the tool predicts results — users with six months or more of tenure show a success rate about 3 to 5 points higher within the same task cluster and delegate less by directive; Anthropic notes learning by doing fits the data but is not proven [8].
    • [8] Anthropic, Anthropic Economic Index report: Learning Curves (March 2026) — https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report
    • target: the closing line
  4. Splitting tasks lengthens what you can finish — users split tasks into steps and pick tasks they expect to work, which accounts for part of the long task lengths seen in practice [9].
    • [9] Anthropic, Economic Index: New building blocks for AI use (January 2026) — https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-primitives
    • target: the break-down row
