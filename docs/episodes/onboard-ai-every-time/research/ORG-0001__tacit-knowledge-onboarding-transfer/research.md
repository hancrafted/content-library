# Why does tacit organisational knowledge fail to transfer during employee onboarding?

## Context

Asked to support the claim that a newcomer, human or AI, lacks what the organisation knows but has never written down. The question is about knowledge that stays with its holders, and why handing a newcomer the documents does not hand over that knowledge.

## Answer

Tacit knowledge fails to transfer because its holders cannot fully state it. Skills and mental models are held so deeply that the holder often cannot say what they know, so a handbook carries only the explicit part. The newcomer must also bring prior knowledge to absorb it, and the knowledge is passed on mostly through observation, imitation and practice with the holder, which an onboarding that stops at documents does not supply. Researchers dispute whether tacit knowledge can be converted into explicit form at all, so the safe claim is that writing down narrows the gap and does not close it.

## Findings

1. Nonaka defines tacit knowledge as "highly personal", "hard to formalize and, therefore, difficult to communicate to others", and quotes Polanyi: "We can know more than we can tell." [1]
2. Part of it is skill: "informal, hard-to-pin-down skills" captured in the term "know-how". A master craftsman "is often unable to articulate the scientific or technical principles behind what he knows." [1]
3. Part of it is mental models, beliefs and perspectives "so ingrained that we take them for granted and therefore cannot easily articulate them", and these "profoundly shape how we perceive the world around us." [1]
4. In Nonaka's example, Matsushita's engineers could not obtain "any meaningful data" from X-rays of dough kneaded by professional bakers. A software developer, Ikuko Tanaka, learned the head baker's technique by apprenticing herself to him, which took "observation, imitation, and practice", and a year of trial and error with the engineers before the technique was specified. [1]
5. Passing tacit knowledge from person to person works by socialization, and Nonaka notes its limit: "neither the apprentice nor the master gains any systematic insight into their craft knowledge", and because it never becomes explicit, it "cannot easily be leveraged by the organization as a whole." [1]
6. In 122 best-practice transfers between units in eight companies, Szulanski found that knowledge-related barriers dominate motivational ones: the recipient's lack of absorptive capacity (weight 0.54), causal ambiguity (0.34) and an arduous source-recipient relationship (0.33). He ties causal ambiguity to tacitness, citing Polanyi's "undefinable portion of knowledge" embodied in "highly tacit human skills". [2]
7. Szulanski defines absorptive capacity as the recipient's ability "to value, assimilate and apply new knowledge", largely "a function of their preexisting stock of knowledge". A newcomer, who has the least organisation-specific prior knowledge, is the extreme case. [2]
8. Causal ambiguity means the reasons for success cannot be determined "even ex post", so no "unambiguous list of the factors of production" exists to hand over. An arduous relationship (laborious and distant) matters most when the knowledge "has tacit components" and "may require numerous individual exchanges". [2]
9. The dispute: Gourlay argues that Nonaka's claim that knowledge is created by converting between tacit and explicit forms is flawed, that no evidence for the four conversion modes could not be explained more simply, and that the framework omits knowledge that is inherently tacit. [3]
10. The neighbouring research file reports the same gap from the AI side: a newcomer such as an AI has broad ability but no knowledge of the organisation's norms, so someone must supply context, a brief and checks. It also records that anything left unwritten is gone for an AI, which does not learn between sessions. [4]

## Caveats

- Szulanski studied transfers of practices between units, not new hires. Applying his barriers to onboarding is an inference; the newcomer's missing prior knowledge fits his absorptive-capacity barrier, but no source here measured onboarding.
- Nonaka's article is an essay built on Japanese company cases, not a measured study; it describes tacit knowledge and does not test why transfer fails. Szulanski's design is correlational and its sample excludes problematic or aborted transfers, which he flags as survival bias.
- Gourlay [3] is read from the abstract on the publisher DOI and its repository page; his arguments were not read in full, and Finding 9 rests on that abstract.
- Whether writing can ever fully capture tacit knowledge is contested [3]. The Answer therefore claims only that documents carry the explicit part, not that the rest can never be written.
- Finding 10 carries claims from an earlier file that cites vendor and practitioner sources [4]; they are advice, not measurement.
