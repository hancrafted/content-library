# Treat AI like a brilliant freelancer with amnesia {#freelancer-with-amnesia}

## The thought experiment {#the-thought-experiment}

#### Voice script

1. **Hook, new job** — Imagine you start a new job. New environment, new culture, new tools, and on your first week someone hands you a task: organise next year's summer party for 200 employees, spread across Europe. (pause) Before you book anything, picture every question you would need answered to do that well, because you are about to hear how many there are.
2. **What this is, and is not** — This is episode one of three on working with AI in an agentic way, and it is built as the reference the later videos point back to. I will use plain words and no jargon, because you do not need to know how AI works inside to get reliable results from it. One thing I leave out on purpose: the context and memory features of ChatGPT and similar tools. They deserve their own episode, the one on attention span, so we keep them out of this one.
3. **Where it goes** — Here is the point of the next ten minutes: everything you just pictured, the questions, the history, the unwritten rules, is what AI needs from you too, because what is good for a human is also good for AI. So we take it in two steps, two cornerstones. First, who you are really handing the work to, and then what you give them so the work comes back right.

### You're the new hire {#you-are-the-new-hire}

#### Speaker notes

1. **Why the middle zone stays unwritten** — Nonaka calls this tacit knowledge: "highly personal", "hard to formalize and, therefore, difficult to communicate to others"; in Polanyi's words, "We can know more than we can tell" [1].
   - sources: [1] Ikujiro Nonaka, The Knowledge-Creating Company, Harvard Business Review (Nov-Dec 1991; reprinted as an HBR Classic, Jul-Aug 2007) — https://hbr.org/2007/07/the-knowledge-creating-company
2. **The newcomer has the least to absorb with** — in 122 best-practice transfers between units in eight companies, knowledge-related barriers outweighed motivational ones; the recipient's lack of absorptive capacity weighed most (0.54), ahead of causal ambiguity (0.34) [2].
   - sources: [2] Gabriel Szulanski, Exploring Internal Stickiness: Impediments to the Transfer of Best Practice Within the Firm, Strategic Management Journal 17 (1996) — https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.4250171105
3. **Even the experts cannot always say it** — a master baker's technique could not be read off X-rays of the dough; it took a developer's apprenticeship ("observation, imitation, and practice") and a year of trial and error before it was specified [1].
   - sources: [1] Ikujiro Nonaka, The Knowledge-Creating Company, Harvard Business Review (Nov-Dec 1991; reprinted as an HBR Classic, Jul-Aug 2007) — https://hbr.org/2007/07/the-knowledge-creating-company
4. **One caution on the theory** — Gourlay disputes Nonaka's account of knowledge being created by converting between tacit and explicit forms; the claim here rests on the definition of tacit knowledge, not on that conversion model [3].
   - sources: [3] Stephen Gourlay, Conceptualizing Knowledge Creation: A Critique of Nonaka's Theory, Journal of Management Studies 43(7) (2006) — https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6486.2006.00637.x

#### Voice script

1. **Obvious questions** — Start with the questions that come to mind at once. What is the budget, where does everyone stay, who signs off on the plan? Dietary and accessibility needs ride along with those. These are easy to list and easy to get answered, which is exactly why they are not where a plan goes wrong.
2. **The ones you forget** — The trouble sits in the second group. What went wrong at last year's party, which unwritten rules apply, which suppliers and tools you are allowed to use? That context lives in feedback, in the culture and in habit, not in any document, so people forget to hand it over, because nobody ever wrote it down. (pause) And you cannot ask for what you do not know exists.
3. **Switch of roles** — Now flip it. You are the one handing the party over, and your boss expects the plan by Friday. The question is no longer what you need to ask, but what the newcomer needs from you, and how much of that you would think to say. That works for any newcomer, which brings us to the one you are about to hire.

#### Speaker notes

1. **Right or not, the output looks finished** — even state-of-the-art models "still regularly produce logical mistakes", which is why speed without checking is a trap [1].
   - sources: [1] Lightman et al., Let's Verify Step by Step — https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050
2. **Probable, not random** — text generation samples from a probability distribution rather than always picking the single likeliest word, because the always-likeliest text is bland and repetitive [2]. Even with randomness set to zero the provider documents that "the results will not be fully deterministic" [3].
   - sources: [2] Holtzman et al., The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration — https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09751
   - sources: [3] Anthropic, Messages API reference (temperature parameter) — https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/messages
3. **How much varies** — in one test 1,000 completions of one prompt at zero randomness gave 80 unique answers [4]. Across five models set up for determinism, accuracy moved by up to 15% between runs of the same task [5].
   - sources: [4] He et al., Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference — https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/
   - sources: [5] Atil et al., Non-Determinism of "Deterministic" LLM Settings — https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667
4. **The bill covers the whole chat** — the interface is stateless: the earlier turns go in with every request, and the model's own answers become part of the next input [6]. Each resent word is billed as input [7].
   - sources: [6] Anthropic, Context windows — https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/context-windows
   - sources: [7] Anthropic, Pricing — https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing
5. **A discount, not an exemption** — cached reads cost about a tenth of base input, the default cache lasts 5 minutes, and caching changes what is paid, not whether the words count toward the window [8].
   - sources: [8] Anthropic, Prompt caching — https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching
6. **Why it guesses instead of asking** — most benchmarks score a guess above an abstention, so training rewards guessing [9]. Current models rarely ask for clarification and answer wrongly instead [10].
   - sources: [9] Kalai et al., Why Language Models Hallucinate — https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664
   - sources: [10] Kuhn et al., CLAM: Selective Clarification for Ambiguous Questions with Generative Language Models — https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.07769
7. **Why it is blind to your company** — a model's accuracy on a fact relates to how many documents about it it saw in training [11], and a leading model family was trained on publicly available data alone [12]. The provider states it does not train on commercial inputs and outputs by default [13].
   - sources: [11] Kandpal et al., Large Language Models Struggle to Learn Long-Tail Knowledge — https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.08411
   - sources: [12] Touvron et al., LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models — https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13971
   - sources: [13] Anthropic, Is my data used for model training? (commercial products) — https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996868-is-my-data-used-for-model-training
8. **The forgetting is in the design** — the context window is described as the model's "working memory", distinct from the corpus it was trained on [6]. Memory across chats exists only where an application stores notes and puts them back, since the memory tool runs client-side [14].
   - sources: [6] Anthropic, Context windows — https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/context-windows
   - sources: [14] Anthropic, Memory tool — https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/memory-tool

#### Voice script

1. **Who gets the job** — So who are you handing the work to? Not a chatbot and not an oracle, but a hire: a brilliant freelancer with amnesia, every time. They come with five traits, and the first letters spell PRICE, because if you do not manage them, you pay. For now I only describe the freelancer, a person, so you can picture them.
2. **Probabilistic, Relentless** — The first trait is Probabilistic. Give the same brief to a new freelancer and you get a different plan: each one picks a likely plan, not a fixed one, which means one good result is not a promise. The second is Relentless. You hand over the job and they work day and night, producing stacks of polished pages, whether the pages are right or not, so there is a lot to read and all of it needs checking.
3. **Informed** — Third, Informed. They know every trade and give expert answers, yet they are blind to your company and your culture. The handbook they get is redacted, sometimes on purpose, and sometimes because nobody, not even the person onboarding them, knows what is missing.
4. **Confident, Every word billed** — Fourth, Confident. You give a vague request, they start without asking, fill the gaps with their own assumptions and sell the result to you as right. A guess sounds exactly as sure as a fact. And fifth, Every word billed: this freelancer bills for every word they read and write, so one more question from you means paying for them to reread the whole file, their own pages included. The longer the job runs, the bigger the bill, and the bill grows faster than the work does.
5. **Amnesia** — And here is what sits under all five. Every time you come back, they forget everything, so every job starts from day one. (pause) Hold that picture of the freelancer, because the next thing I show is who this really is.
6. **AI lands last** — This freelancer is AI. Row by row, it is the same sentence with different nouns. Give the same prompt in a new chat and you get a different answer. Send the prompt and it produces pages in seconds, as many as you ask for, right or not. Trained on more text than anyone could read, it gives you the likeliest answer, blind to your company and culture. Give it a vague prompt and it fills the gaps with the most common answers and replies with full confidence. And it charges for every word it reads and writes, so one more message pays for the whole chat again, its own answers included. (pause) Would you hand a job to a freelancer like that without a plan?
7. **The pun** — So here is the whole slide in one line. Forget the price, and you'll pay it every time.

#### Speaker notes

1. **Break it down** — least-to-most prompting, solving simpler subproblems in sequence, scored 99% or better on a compositional benchmark against 16% for chain-of-thought prompting with an older model [1]. Models can answer every sub-question and still fail the whole one, and that gap does not shrink as models grow [2].
   - sources: [1] Zhou et al., Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in Large Language Models — https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10625
   - sources: [2] Press et al., Measuring and Narrowing the Compositionality Gap in Language Models — https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03350
2. **Requirements first** — across prompts that each stated a different subset of 20 requirements, results varied more than twice as much for a requirement left unstated as for one stated: standard deviation 8.9% against under half that [3].
   - sources: [3] Yang, Shi, Ma, Liu, Kästner, Wu, What Prompts Don't Say: Understanding and Managing Underspecification in LLM Prompts (v3) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13360
3. **Interview yourself with AI** — agents that interacted with the user to fill missing information improved performance by up to 74% on an underspecified coding benchmark [4]. When a model elicits preferences with open-ended questions, users' answers were often more informative than prompts they wrote unaided [5].
   - sources: [4] Vijayvargiya et al., Ambig-SWE: Interactive Agents to Overcome Underspecificity in Software Engineering — https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13069
   - sources: [5] Li et al., Eliciting Human Preferences with Language Models — https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11589
4. **Evaluate each step** — on a maths benchmark, feedback on each reasoning step "significantly outperforms" feedback on the final result alone [6]. Models also "struggle to self-correct their responses without external feedback", so the check needs something outside the model, here the requirements [7].
   - sources: [6] Lightman et al., Let's Verify Step by Step — https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050
   - sources: [7] Huang et al., Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet — https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798
5. **Fresh start for each piece** — across six generation tasks, top models scored on average 39% lower in multi-turn conversation than with the full instruction at once; the authors advise starting a new conversation that repeats the information [8]. Performance also degrades with input length across all 18 models tested [9].
   - sources: [8] Laban, Hayashi, Zhou, Neville, LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation — https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120
   - sources: [9] Hong, Troynikov, Huber, Context Rot: How Increasing Input Tokens Impacts LLM Performance — https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot
6. **A limit on Break it down** — decomposition does not make a model reliable at composing the pieces; errors in early steps still carry into later ones, which is why the next move is checking [10].
   - sources: [10] Dziri et al., Faith and Fate: Limits of Transformers on Compositionality — https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18654

#### Voice script

1. **One prompt is not enough** — Many people hope for one good prompt. But a good prompt is not a brief. A brief is a set of moves, and each move answers a cost you have just seen, so one prompt cannot do the work of five.
2. **Break it down, Requirements first** — The first move is Break it down: hand over one piece at a time, because small pieces leave less to get wrong and less to pay for. That answers Relentless and Every word billed, the pages and the bloated bill. The second is Requirements first: write down what good looks like before the work starts. Then the same brief brings results that agree, so it answers Probabilistic and Confident.
3. **Interview, Evaluate** — Third, Interview yourself with AI. Let it ask you its questions first, so the confident guess becomes a question, and the informed outsider learns the company it was blind to. Fourth, Evaluate each step, and here evaluating means checking the step against your requirements. A wrong turn is then caught early, not after the last page, which tames the flood of pages and the varying answers.
4. **Fresh start** — Fifth, Fresh start for each piece. One piece gets one clean chat, so the bill stays small and nothing old leaks in from earlier work. Notice that the map is many to many: one move helps several traits, and one trait is met by several moves. That overlap is why the five work as a set.
5. **Refrain** — Look at what these moves are: a brief you would give any good freelancer. Smaller pieces, written requirements, questions up front, checks along the way, a clean desk for each job. What is good for a human is also good for AI.

#### Speaker notes

1. **Why the sign-off is a gate** — stating every requirement at once is not free: one requirement stated alone was followed 98.7% of the time, but with 19 stated gpt-4o fell to 85.0%, part of it from conflicts between requirements [1]. A short card of essentials is the reason.
   - sources: [1] Yang, Shi, Ma, Liu, Kästner, Wu, What Prompts Don't Say: Understanding and Managing Underspecification in LLM Prompts (v3) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13360
2. **A wrong early guess sticks** — when instructions were revealed piece by piece, models scored 39% lower on average, mostly as unreliability; they "make assumptions in early turns" and do not recover from a wrong turn [2].
   - sources: [2] Laban, Hayashi, Zhou, Neville, LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation — https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120
3. **Focus beats volume** — on a long-memory benchmark, prompts holding only the relevant material, only a few hundred words of relevant material scored significantly higher than full prompts of tens of thousands of words, and even a single distractor lowered results [3].
   - sources: [3] Hong, Troynikov, Huber, Context Rot: How Increasing Input Tokens Impacts LLM Performance — https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot
4. **Plan, then fresh session** — after interviewing for a spec, the vendor's own guidance is to "start a fresh session to execute it", since the new session "has clean context focused entirely on implementation" [4].
   - sources: [4] Anthropic, Best practices for Claude Code — https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices
5. **The answer can be right and the work wrong** — among solutions with a correct final answer, the best system cut reasoning errors from 14.0% to 3.4%, so a check on the result alone accepts flawed reasoning [5].
   - sources: [5] Uesato et al., Solving math word problems with process- and outcome-based feedback — https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14275

#### Voice script

1. **Restate** — The task: plan the summer party for 200 people across Europe. If you asked AI for all of that in one prompt, you would get pages of polished and confident answers, and you could trust almost none of them.
2. **Break it down** — So first, break it down. The party falls into five pieces: venue, travel, catering, invitations and budget. We take only the venue now, and the other four wait for their turn, because one piece is small enough to get right.
3. **Interview** — Next, the interview. Before it starts, the AI asks you questions, the way a contact at an agency would. Three questions come up, and one of them is something you had forgotten: whether last year's party left feedback worth keeping. That is the unwritten context coming out before the AI guesses.
4. **Requirements** — Then requirements, and this is a stage you sign off before any research starts. Everything gets written onto one card, short and made of essentials, and every later job reads from it. If the card is wrong, you want to find out here, while it is cheap.
5. **Research, fresh each time** — Now the research. Three venues get three fresh chats, and each one is checked against the card. Handing work to several of them at once is its own topic, and it is the promotion Episode.
6. **Compare and check** — The three results come back together and are compared, again against the card, not against a feeling. That makes two gates in the whole flow: the card, and the comparison. A mistake has to get past both.
7. **Recommendation, and on** — What comes out is the venue plan for your boss, and the whole thing ran on the same BRIEF you would use for any task. (pause) Now let's do it live: I will show you how that interview feels when you run it yourself.
